A crash year costs 12 times more in the last year of the plan

Sequence risk gets argued as a retirement problem: retire, the market drops, the first withdrawals sell shares at the bottom. The same arithmetic runs on the way in. A 15-year savings plan is a short runway, and on a short runway a drawdown in the final years lands on the biggest balance with the least time to heal. Put numbers on it: 66,000 saved at the start of every year for 15 years, growing 7 percent. The finish line is $1,774,612. The final five years build $798,894 of that, 45 percent of the whole plan, which is why a bad year there is a different event than a bad year anywhere else.

Take one 30 percent crash year, contributions unchanged, everything else still 7 percent. In year 1 the crash lands on a base of $66,000 and the hole at the horizon is 19,800 x 1.07^14 = $51,055. In year 15 it lands on $1,658,515 and the same 30 percent removes 0.30 x $1,658,515 = $497,555 off the top. The plan ends at $1,160,961 instead of $1,774,612, a $613,651 shortfall that is 12.0 times the year-1 hole. Refilling it at 66,000 a year takes 9.3 more years of saving. Same crash, same market, and the date moves the bill by an order of magnitude.

Concentration sets the size of that bill. The Magnificent Seven have run to roughly a third of the S&P 500's total market cap, so a cap-weighted fund hands them 30 percent of the money. A 50 percent mega-cap drawdown with the rest flat lifts a 15 percent portfolio hit out of that weight alone, $248,777 on the year-15 base, and it lands hardest exactly where the calendar already hurts. An equal-weight fund caps any single name at a fifth of a percent, so the same event moves the portfolio 0.70 percent, $11,609, about 21 times smaller. Equal weight is a sequencing hedge on a short clock, and its price is knowable: the expense ratio runs about 15 basis points higher, $1,121 a year on average across this plan, $16,813 over the whole run.

The comparison closes itself. The total premium for the structural hedge is $16,813 over 15 years. The single worst-day scenario it trims is a $248,777 hit, 15 times the premium on its own, sitting inside a crash whose placement inside the final five years is what turns a market event into a plan failure. Diversification that only argues about long-run returns misses the mechanism: the plan spends its final years holding the most money at the highest stakes, and the weights you carry into those years are chosen in advance or not at all.
